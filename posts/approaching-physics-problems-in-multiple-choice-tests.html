Approaching Physics problems in Multiple choice tests

Problem solving methods, MCQ

        First off, get really really good at algebra and algebraic manipulations. Your "number sense" will be the key factor in determining your speed. Questions like 2x-5=13 should take <2s to solve or get an intuition for where the answer should lie on the number line. This type of intuition can only be built through repetition in the hundreds at minimum. By solving an enormous amount of questions, you create a reference database from which you can approximate answers to any new, yet similar questions which greatly improve your speed and increase confidence when hunting for the answer. Also, the values of constants and their integer multiples of 2, 3 are quite useful when looking for probable answers. 

       Now, most of you are likely studying physics for the first time, and first time only, just like how the syllabus plans. But learning a concept once is nowhere near enough to master it; so I highly recommend going ahead of schedule and learn all topics in your syllabus at least twice from the ground up before your exams. The first time allows you to understand the general gist of what's going on, while more repetitions make sure you pick up any tiny details and conditions that you left behind [these are what the exam is usually testing you on]. Those tiny details are what most students get stuck on; typically a single keyword or phrase that separates the absolute correct answer from something that somewhat fits your intuition but is actually inaccurate. By increasing the repetition of learning and revision, your confidence in your physical intuition increases; which leads to higher scores (Let's say you "know" the topic the question is about. Then, 10 questions answered at 50% certainty will yield around 5 points or worse, while absolute confidence means you get all 10 points).

       Next, you need remove all language barriers. Whatever language you are studying in, there is bound to be some words that are similar enough in meaning for regular usage, but represent different ideas in physics (or any field). Take for example, luminance and luminosity. Luminance is light leaving or reflecting off a surface toward the eye, while luminosity is the total intrinsic energy/light output of a source per unit time. Also, certain phrasing, like per unit time instead of per second, are worth keeping an eye out for, as they are totally not necessary for any real physics you'll end up doing. But they are indeed the correct and accurate way to describe phenomena. Another thing to be aware of is notations, but those are becoming standardised at a rapid pace so it's not significant. Additionally, the popular textbooks you'll likely learn from are properly notated. 

        Now that your pre-requisites are covered, let's talk about the methods. Tests and examinations aim to challenge your knowledge of physics. But the tests being standardised and there being only so many different types of questions mean that developing tactics and methods that bypass the need to know actual physics is possible. Thankfully, newly developed tests are starting to challenge actual knowledge, but we can still utilise some shorthanded methods to ballpark the answer without doing all the working necessary for an open question. 

        Questions involving proportionality or simple product formulas can be easily checked by mental calculation, especially if the 2-3 significant figures of the answers are unique (don't calculate orders of magnitude). And pay extra attention to wording. 

        Additionally, highlight or underline words about direction, categories etc. Subtle differences in wording can require entirely different approaches, both mathematically and physically, leading to answers that may not even be within the choices given. I'm sure you know about the obvious tips, like crossing out obviously wrong answers, checking for similarity between choices [this is mostly useless nowadays unless your teacher is very old fashioned or lazy], and of course, gambling between the likely [or non-unlikely] answers. Speaking of gambling, don't choose what 'seems' right. If you're not immediately sure of the answer, then your intuition and knowledge about that topic is incomplete or wrong, meaning the option you see as more 'correct' is actually way more likely to be wrong. So I recommend you choose fully randomly, or at least independent of your knowledge of physics. I recommend spinning a coin or seeking help from the random number generator on your calculator. 

        Another skill you should develop from doing many problems is physical approximation. For example, if an object has 10^4 m/s^2 acceleration, then you are bound to recheck your working, or if a charge has charge more than 1 coulomb (which is a LOT of charge). Given the context of the question, some choices are just too far fetched for reality, so you can safely cross those out. If a formula has squares, roots, or other fractional powers, then you can use the conditions for those to quickly check if some answers are valid. 

        That really is it. To improve your accuracy and speed further, you need better mastery of topics, derivation and manipulation of formulas and relationships, why said formulas are true and under what conditions. In general, think of learning as being equal to maintaining, and expanding a library. When you encounter something unknown, you scramble for knowledge from multiple sources, which is slow and not very reliable. But after you acquire the book [solution], you can quickly reference it later. And not revising means your 'books' will get weathered and wear away. How fast you can recall previous knowledge can be trained if you try hard. which is hard as your brain absolutely detests working. But alas, nothing in life comes easy, and there is great joy to be found in solving humanities problems one by one, even if your body hates it.
